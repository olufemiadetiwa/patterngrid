# Pattern Grid service pages expansion prompt

Apply this content and interaction expansion to the current Pattern Grid website. It adds substance to the Services overview, all five service detail pages and their related sections across the site. Use it alongside the visual upgrade prompt: retain Geist typography, photographic hero sliders, short hero headlines and the scroll-highlighted introductions.

Every service page must explain what the client is trying to achieve, what Pattern Grid does, what the client receives, how the engagement progresses, which technologies may be involved and how information moves through the solution. Use the exact proposed copy below. Build the diagrams and deliverable previews as real visual components, not descriptions of visuals that are never implemented.

## 01 The new standard for service pages

Turn each service page into a substantial consulting proposition. A short introduction, four deliverable labels and a generic process are insufficient. The reader should leave understanding both the business value and the work required to achieve it.

Use this sequence on each detail page:

1. Photographic slider with the existing short headline.
2. Large scroll-highlighted statement followed by a concise service introduction.
3. Recognisable business situations and the outcomes to work towards.
4. Six concrete deliverables, each with a meaningful icon and a useful description.
5. A service-specific approach showing activities, outputs and decision points.
6. A large system, decision or information-flow illustration with relevant branches and feedback.
7. A worked example or an inspectable sample deliverable.
8. Relevant tools and technologies, each explained by its role.
9. How success can be measured, related expertise and focused FAQs.
10. A service-specific consultation invitation.

Give the page visual variety. Use open editorial layouts, an icon-led deliverables section, a dark illustrated system, a light sample-artifact section and a restrained technology band. Preserve space around each composition. Do not put every paragraph inside a card or turn the page into a wall of technical labels.

On desktop, allow a quiet in-page navigation strip after the introduction: Overview, Deliverables, Approach, Technology and Questions. On mobile, use a compact jump menu. The page remains readable without opening any navigation controls.

The six deliverables below describe a typical scope, not an unconditional bundle. Use this shared public line once near the deliverables: **“Deliverables are agreed around your priorities, systems and starting point.”** Keep commercial details, dates and quantified promises out of the page unless approved.

## 02 Icons technology and visual components

### Deliverable icons

Use one consistent family of original SVG line icons. The included icon sprite provides the IDs used in this brief. Use a 24-unit drawing grid, rounded line caps, approximately 1.75-unit strokes and no decorative gradients. Show icons at 28–36 px, with navy on light sections and pale teal on dark sections.

Each deliverable needs an icon, a short title, a 20–35 word description and, where useful, a small preview of the actual kind of artifact being described. Make the deliverable concrete: a roadmap, tested pipeline, metric dictionary, workflow, runbook or skills plan.

Use a three-column by two-row arrangement on wide screens, two columns on tablets and one column on mobile. Keep all six descriptions visible. An optional “View example” button may open a larger illustrative artifact; hover must never be the only way to discover what is included.

Icons accompany text. Hide decorative SVGs from assistive technology, and give standalone icon buttons an accessible name. Do not use emoji, inconsistent icon packs or large gradient squares behind every symbol.

### Tools and technology

Show the role of a technology rather than a decorative wall of logos. Each entry has a genuine product mark where approved, the correct product name and one plain sentence explaining its use. Technologies should sit below the approach and demonstration, where readers have enough context to understand them.

The business brief names **Microsoft Fabric, Azure, SQL, Power BI and Excel**. These form the initial technology vocabulary. DAX and Power Query are relevant technical components for the Power BI examples. Additional products proposed below are architecture options; confirm delivery capability before presenting them as Pattern Grid's established expertise.

Use **“Technology shaped around your environment”** as the section heading, with this introduction: **“We select tools around the problem, your existing estate and the people who will operate the solution.”**

Keep a private content field separating tools named in the business brief from proposed additions. Publish confirmed entries, and retain the rest as implementation options until verified. Do not display internal verification notes to visitors. Do not imply vendor partnership, certification or endorsement through logos or badges.

Use official logo assets without redrawing or arbitrarily recolouring them. If an approved mark is unavailable, use a clean text label. SQL is a language: use a database icon rather than inventing a SQL vendor logo. APIs, data modelling and governance are concepts, not software brands.

### Two different kinds of illustration

The **approach** explains how the consulting work is delivered. Each stage shows its output and the decision needed before proceeding. The **information flow** explains how business information becomes an answer, an action or evidence of capability. They must not be the same five-box strip with different headings.

Use the supplied service illustration boards as editable visual references. Their SVG groups separate the approach and the information flow. Rebuild responsive production compositions with readable HTML and SVG labels; do not shrink a desktop diagram into an unreadable mobile image.

Use solid connectors for information moving forward, dashed connectors for review or feedback, and explicitly labelled branches for exceptions. Use colour with labels and shapes, so meaning survives in monochrome. Keep all text visible in the resting state. A selected node may highlight its incoming and outgoing relationships and reveal a short explanation beside the diagram.

Animate one meaningful path at a time. Trace a connection over approximately 600–900 ms, then hold the complete state. Play once when the section enters view, with Replay available. User interaction can step through the diagram. Reduced motion shows the complete static illustration. Avoid decorative particle traffic, perpetual moving numbers and fabricated live connections.

## 05 Data Engineering and Platforms

**Section heading:** Make the path from source to answer dependable.

When reporting depends on manual exports, copied spreadsheets and undocumented fixes, every change creates another opportunity for the numbers to drift. The result is a fragile process that becomes harder to maintain as the business grows.

Pattern Grid designs and builds the data foundations behind reliable intelligence. We connect agreed sources, make transformations traceable, establish quality checks and prepare usable models for reporting and downstream applications. The work includes the operating responsibilities needed to keep the system dependable after implementation.

Pattern Grid brings metric design, data modelling and reporting into one connected piece of work. ## 07 AI and Automation

**Route:** `/services/ai-automation`

**Hero:** Put intelligence to work.

**Scroll statement:** Apply AI where it serves a clear purpose. Reduce repetitive work, support better judgement and keep **people in control** of decisions that matter.

### What the page should say

**Section heading:** Build a useful workflow around a well-defined task.

Some work needs a reliable rule. Other work involves interpreting a document, finding relevant information or preparing a draft that a person will review. Choosing the right approach matters more than putting AI into every step.

Pattern Grid helps identify practical automation opportunities, test a bounded solution and connect it to the systems and people involved. We define the inputs, evaluate the output and make the handover between automation and human judgement explicit. A pilot should produce evidence about what works, where it fails and what is required to operate it responsibly.

**This is useful when:** reporting involves repeated manual steps; teams re-enter information between systems; finding internal knowledge takes too long; or an AI idea needs a realistic test before wider deployment.

**Outcome to work towards:** a useful, measurable workflow with an accountable owner, visible exceptions and a tested operating model.

### What you receive

| Deliverable | Icon ID | Proposed public description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opportunity and process assessment | search | A review of the task, existing process, inputs and effort, identifying where rules, integration or AI assistance could make a useful difference. |
| Workflow and control blueprint | workflow | A clear design for triggers, steps, system connections and decision points, including where a person approves, corrects or takes over the work. |
| Bounded pilot or prototype | code | A working implementation of an agreed use case, with defined inputs and limits so its behaviour can be evaluated before wider deployment decisions. |
| Evaluation and test pack | clipboard | Representative test cases and results covering output quality, failure modes and operating requirements, with an explicit basis for deciding what happens next. |
| Integration and review controls | shield | The agreed connections, access boundaries, approval steps and exception routes that make the workflow usable within the surrounding business process. |
| Operating and improvement guide | book | Guidance for monitoring the workflow, reviewing outputs, handling failures and managing changes, with ownership and an improvement backlog clearly documented. |

**Artifact preview:** show a real layout for a work item: Input, Supporting sources, Proposed output, Checks, Review and Final status. A reviewer decision visibly changes the status. The website example remains an illustration and does not submit information to an external system.

### How we work

1. **Select.** Establish the task, process owner and baseline effort. Output: a bounded use-case brief.
2. **Design.** Separate rule-based steps from AI-assisted steps and specify the controls. Output: a workflow blueprint and evaluation plan.
3. **Prototype.** Build against suitable representative inputs. Output: a pilot with explicit limitations.
4. **Evaluate.** Test normal cases, difficult cases and failures with users. Output: results and a proceed, revise or stop decision.
5. **Integrate.** Connect the approved workflow to its operating environment. Output: integration, monitoring and an accountable handover.

Show an explicit loop from Evaluate back to Prototype. A successful demo is not equivalent to a production-ready system. The page should explain the evidence needed to move between them without overwhelming the reader with a control checklist.

### Information and action flow illustration

**Title:** Evidence before action.

A request or event enters an authorised workflow. Approved business records or documents provide relevant evidence. A rule-based or AI-assisted step prepares the proposed output. Validation checks assess the required fields, references and business rules. An accountable person reviews the proposal and either approves the next action or returns it for correction. The action and review outcome are recorded for monitoring.

Draw source access before evidence retrieval. Label AI output as “Proposed”, not “Verified”. Missing evidence, failed checks or an unauthorised request must not proceed to an approved action. Rejected proposals follow a visible correction path. Never use an invented model confidence percentage as a guarantee of correctness.

**Interaction:** switch between a normal sample and a sample with a missing source. The second follows the exception path and explains what is missing. Show the reviewer approving or returning the item without simulating a live assistant or performing a real business action.

### Technology and working methods

Publish the products confirmed for the actual delivery offering. The following are relevant architecture options, not a statement that every engagement uses them:

| Technology or component | Role when selected |
| --- | --- |
| Azure and SQL | Hosting, integration and access to controlled business records where appropriate. |
| Power Automate | Triggers, connectors and approval steps for supported business workflows. |
| Azure AI Search | Retrieval of relevant indexed information for a knowledge workflow, with access restrictions designed and configured explicitly. |
| Azure Document Intelligence | Extraction of text, tables or fields from suitable documents for downstream processing and validation. |
| Approved model service | A selected model for the bounded interpretation or drafting task, evaluated against the use case rather than chosen for novelty. |
| Application APIs | Controlled connection to the systems where an approved result needs to be used. |

Do not show all these products as a fixed stack. A deterministic reporting workflow may need no language model. A knowledge assistant and a document-processing workflow have different architectures. Name and display a model provider only after it is selected and the public capability is confirmed.

### Example and evidence

**Example question:** Can a weekly performance briefing be assembled with less repeated preparation?

A scheduled process collects approved metrics and checks their reporting period. A draft briefing links statements to the relevant measures. Missing data is flagged. A business owner reviews the draft before it is released. In the illustration, the missing-data example must visibly stop at review rather than producing a polished but unsupported answer.

**Measures to agree:** manual handling time; completion time; proportion of outputs passing the agreed checks; exception rate; reviewer correction effort; operating cost. Compare equivalent tasks and include human review time.

### Questions and next step

**Do we need AI for every automation?** No. Where clear rules and reliable integrations can do the job, those may be the better starting point.

**How are errors handled?** The design includes agreed checks, exception routes and review responsibilities. Evaluation should identify both the useful behaviour and the cases that need intervention.

**What makes a good first pilot?** A bounded task with available information, a clear owner and a practical way to compare the result with the current process.

**Closing headline:** Choose one useful task. Build the evidence.

**CTA:** Explore an automation use case. **Related services:** Data and AI Strategy and Data Engineering.

### How we work

1. **Baseline.** Identify the roles, tasks and current capability. Output: a skills and responsibility map.
2. **Design.** Set role-specific learning outcomes and suitable examples. Output: learning pathways and practical tasks.
3. **Practise.** Facilitate guided work using appropriate data and tools. Output: completed exercises and specific feedback.
4. **Demonstrate.** Assess the agreed tasks through practical work. Output: evidence of independent capability and remaining gaps.
5. **Reinforce.** Connect learning to daily use and support ownership. Output: playbooks, champions and an agreed follow-up plan.

Show the approach as a learning loop. A gap identified during Demonstrate returns to targeted practice. Attendance alone must not automatically move a participant to “Independent”.

### Learning and evidence flow illustration

**Title:** From guided practice to independent capability.

Role requirements and baseline evidence define a tailored practice plan. Practice produces a work sample. The sample is reviewed against the agreed criteria. Demonstrated capability moves into independent workplace use; a remaining gap returns to targeted practice. Evidence from workplace use feeds back into the playbook and future learning priorities.

Use people, task and evidence symbols. This is an information and learning flow, not a technology pipeline. Keep individual assessment results private; the public website uses clearly illustrative roles and tasks.

**Interaction:** select Leader, Analyst or Engineer to change the example task and evidence required. Preserve the same underlying principle: a relevant task, practice, a work sample, review and evidence of independent use.
